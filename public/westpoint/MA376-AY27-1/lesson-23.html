Objectives

  • Apply. Fit a random-intercept model with lme4 and recover the ICC from its variance components.

Model 3: Random Company Effect

Model 2 trusts 4 cadets as much as 120 and says nothing about J5. Model 3 fixes both with one new assumption about the company effects:

  • Model 2: 36 unrelated numbers. Each company’s effect comes only from its own cadets.
  • Model 3: all 36 come from one normal distribution. Companies tell you about each other.
Differences between companies Differences between cadets in a company
Model 2 35 separate \(\beta_j\), no variance one variance, \(\sigma^2\)
Model 3 one variance, \(\sigma^2_u\) one variance, \(\sigma^2\)

\[y_{ij} = \mu + u_j + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad u_j \sim N(0, \sigma^2_u), \qquad \varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\]

  • \(\mu\): the overall average
  • \(u_j\): how far company \(j\) sits from \(\mu\) (like Model 2’s \(\beta_j\), but measured from \(\mu\), not A1)
  • \(\sigma^2_u\): how much companies differ
  • \(\sigma^2\): the variance left over after accounting for company

Fitting It: lmer

lmer() from the lme4 package: linear mixed-effects regression. Same formula as lm(), plus random effects.

Piece of the formula Means Effect
score ~ 1 one overall average, \(\mu\) Fixed: one number, estimated directly (\(\hat\mu\)). Fixed effects in the summary.
(1 | company) an intercept (\(1\)) for each company, drawn from \(N(0, \sigma^2_u)\) Random: estimate their spread (\(\hat\sigma^2_u\)), then predict each \(u_j\). Random effects in the summary.

The Model 3 Formula

Like Models 1 and 2, predict a cadet by dropping \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) (it averages 0):

\[\hat y_{ij} = \hat\mu + \hat u_j = \hat\mu + w_j\,(\bar y_j - \hat\mu)\]

The overall average \(\hat\mu = 399.7\), plus a share \(w_j\) of the company’s gap from it. Multiply out and regroup: \(\hat\mu + w_j\bar y_j - w_j\hat\mu = w_j\,\bar y_j + (1 - w_j)\,\hat\mu\), a blend of the company’s own average and the overall average.

\[w_j = \frac{n_j}{n_j + \lambda}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\hat\sigma^2}{\hat\sigma^2_u} = \frac{1685.3}{125.6} = 13.4\]

  • \(w_j\): the trust in the company’s own average, between 0 (ignore it, Model 1) and 1 (take all of it, Model 2). More cadets (\(n_j\)), more trust.
  • \(\lambda\): cadet noise (Residual) over how much companies really differ (company), both from the summary. Big when cadets vary a lot or companies barely differ, small when companies really differ.
  • Here \(\lambda = 13.4\): a company needs about 13 cadets before its own average gets half the weight.
NoteWhere w, lambda and the two variances come from

The \(\hat u_j\). Lesson 8’s ridge regression. lmer picks the \(u_j\) that minimize

\[\underbrace{\sum_{i,j} (y_{ij} - \mu - u_j)^2}_{\text{fit the cadets}} \;+\; \underbrace{\lambda \sum_j u_j^2}_{\text{penalty: keep each } u_j \text{ near } 0}\]

  • First part: least squares, same as lm. Alone, it gives Model 2: each company its own average.
  • Second part: a ridge penalty that pulls every \(u_j\) toward 0, the overall average. \(\lambda\) sets how hard.

Solve for one company. Only company \(j\)’s cadets involve \(u_j\):

\[f(u_j) = \sum_{i=1}^{n_j} (y_{ij} - \mu - u_j)^2 + \lambda u_j^2\]

1. Take the derivative.

\[f'(u_j) = -2\sum_{i=1}^{n_j} (y_{ij} - \mu - u_j) + 2\lambda u_j\]

2. Set it to 0. The sum of \(n_j\) cadets’ scores is \(n_j \bar y_j\), so \(\sum_i (y_{ij} - \mu - u_j) = n_j(\bar y_j - \mu - u_j)\):

\[n_j(\bar y_j - \mu - u_j) = \lambda u_j\]

3. Solve for \(u_j\).

\[n_j(\bar y_j - \mu) = (n_j + \lambda)\,u_j \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat u_j = \frac{n_j}{n_j + \lambda}\,(\bar y_j - \hat\mu)\]

The trust \(w_j\) times the raw gap, as above.

  • \(\lambda = 0\), no penalty: \(w_j = 1\), \(\hat u_j\) is the whole raw gap. Model 2.
  • \(\lambda\) huge: \(w_j \to 0\), \(\hat u_j \to 0\). Every company is the overall average. Model 1.
  • Model 3 sits between, and \(\lambda\) decides where.

Why \(\lambda\) is the variance ratio. Lesson 8 tuned \(\lambda\) by cross-validation. Here the assumption \(u_j \sim N(0, \sigma^2_u)\) sets it. Model 3’s two assumptions, written as \(-2 \log\) density (constants dropped), have two pieces:

\[\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i,j}(y_{ij} - \mu - u_j)^2}_{\text{cadets: } \varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2)} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma^2_u}\sum_j u_j^2}_{\text{companies: } u_j \sim N(0, \sigma^2_u)}\]

Multiply by \(\sigma^2\): the ridge objective with \(\lambda = \sigma^2 / \sigma^2_u\). Ridge had no rule for \(\lambda\). This assumption gives one.

How lmer finds the two variances. By maximum likelihood, with calculus, like Lesson 9’s logistic regression.

1. Write down how likely the data are for any values of the two variances. Company \(j\)’s average has variance \(v_j = \sigma^2_u + \sigma^2 / n_j\): real company spread plus the luck of \(n_j\) cadets. The maximum likelihood (ML) log-likelihood:

\[\ell(\mu, \sigma^2_u, \sigma^2) = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_j \left[ (n_j - 1)\log\sigma^2 + \frac{SS_j}{\sigma^2} + \log v_j + \frac{(\bar y_j - \mu)^2}{v_j} \right] + \text{const}\]

\(SS_j\): the sum of squares of cadets around their own company average.

2. Take the derivative and set it to 0.

\[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma^2_u} = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_j \left[ \frac{1}{v_j} - \frac{(\bar y_j - \mu)^2}{v_j^2} \right] = 0\]

3. No closed-form answer. \(\sigma^2_u\) sits inside every \(v_j\), and each company has its own \(n_j\). You cannot solve it by hand. The computer climbs the hill, as glm did in Lesson 9. lmer uses REML, a version of maximum likelihood with a small correction for estimating \(\mu\): it adds \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\log \sum_j 1/v_j\) to \(\ell\). The hill below is REML.

4. Read off the top. \(\hat\sigma^2_u = 125.6\) and \(\hat\sigma^2 = 1685.3\).

D3: \(n_j = 4\), \(\bar y_j = 453.0\).

\[w = \frac{4}{4 + 13.4} = 0.23, \qquad \hat y = 399.7 + 0.23\,(453.0 - 399.7) = 411.9\]

H2: \(n_j = 120\), \(\bar y_j = 414.3\).

\[w = \frac{120}{120 + 13.4} = 0.90, \qquad \hat y = 399.7 + 0.90\,(414.3 - 399.7) = 412.9\]

  • D3: 4 cadets, \(w = 0.23\). Keeps 23% of its gap.
  • H2: 120 cadets, \(w = 0.90\). Keeps 90% of its gap.

A new company, J5: no cadets, so no gap to trust. \(\hat u_{J5} = 0\) and \(\hat y = 399.7\).

Why shrink? A 4-cadet average is off by about 20.5 points from luck alone; companies really differ by about 11.2. Mostly luck, so D3 keeps 23% of its gap. H2’s 120 cadets: luck about 3.7, so it keeps 90%. The larger the sample, the more we trust the company average and the less we shrink.

The same \(w_j\) two ways. Divide top and bottom by \(n_j\) and put in \(\lambda = \hat\sigma^2 / \hat\sigma^2_u\): real spread over real spread plus luck.

\[w_j = \frac{n_j}{n_j + \lambda} = \frac{\hat\sigma^2_u}{\hat\sigma^2_u + \hat\sigma^2 / n_j}\]

\[w_{D3} = \frac{4}{4 + 13.4} = \frac{11.2^2}{11.2^2 + 20.5^2} = 0.23\]

How Much Does Company Matter? The ICC

From Model 3’s two variances, \(\hat\sigma^2_u = 125.6\) (company) and \(\hat\sigma^2 = 1685.3\) (cadet):

ImportantThe ICC

\[\text{ICC} = \frac{\hat\sigma^2_u}{\hat\sigma^2_u + \hat\sigma^2} = \frac{125.6}{125.6 + 1685.3} = 0.069\]

Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC): Lesson 22’s share of variance from the groups, now read straight off lmer’s two variances.

  • 0: the company tells you nothing. Every company looks the same.
  • 1: everyone in a company scores the same. All the variation is company to company.
  • 7% of the variation in a cadet’s score comes from the company. The rest is cadet to cadet.
  • Also a correlation: two cadets in the same company have scores correlated at 0.07.
  • So cadets in a company are not independent. Model 1 assumes they are, so its standard errors are too small.
NoteWhy the ICC is a correlation

Two cadets in the same company share \(u_j\):

\[y_{1j} = \mu + u_j + \varepsilon_{1j}, \qquad y_{2j} = \mu + u_j + \varepsilon_{2j}\]

\[\text{Cor}(y_{1j}, y_{2j}) = \frac{\text{Cov}(y_{1j}, y_{2j})}{\text{Var}(y_{1j})} = \frac{\sigma^2_u}{\sigma^2_u + \sigma^2} = \text{ICC}\]

The ICC Sets the Pull

\[\lambda = \frac{\hat\sigma^2}{\hat\sigma^2_u} = \frac{1 - \text{ICC}}{\text{ICC}} = \frac{1 - 0.069}{0.069} = 13.4\]

Tell the Commander

Company matters, but not much: 7% of the variation in AFT scores is company to company, and most company averages sit within about 22 points of 400. Do not rank companies on a handful of scores. D3 tops the raw board on 4 cadets, and its estimate drops to 412 once you account for how little 4 scores tell you. On the evidence so far, B1 is the most fit company.

Fixed or Random?

Model 2 treats company as a fixed effect. Model 3 treats it as a random effect.

Fixed Random
The levels the only ones you care about a sample of many
Companies these 36 companies are all that matter these 36 stand in for companies in general, including J5, a company not in the data
The question is this level different? how much do levels vary?
Companies is D3 better than B1? how much do companies differ?
Estimates one effect per level one variance
Companies 36 company averages \(\hat\sigma^2_u = 125.6\), companies differ by about 11 points (SD)
Small groups taken at face value pulled toward the overall average
Pick it when few levels, every one well sampled many levels, or some thinly sampled, even when you want to rank them

Mixed model: some effects fixed, some random. Model 3 is already one: \(\mu\) fixed (the Fixed effects block), company random. Lesson 24 adds fixed predictors.

9. A state inspector asks, “Is Lac Qui Parle worse than Blue Earth?” Fixed or random?

NoteAnswer 9

Random. There are 85 counties, and 11 of them tested 2 homes or fewer. Even to rank them, shrink first. A fixed effect would take Lac Qui Parle’s 2 homes at face value.

ImportantTakeaway
  • Grouped data, three choices: ignore the group, a dummy for each group, or a random intercept.
  • Dummies take every group’s average at face value, so small groups land at the extremes by luck.
  • A random intercept blends each group’s own average with the overall average. Small groups get pulled toward the overall average.
  • Fixed: you care about these levels. Random: the levels are a sample, and you care about how much they vary.
  • The ICC is the share of the variance between groups. It sets the pull.
