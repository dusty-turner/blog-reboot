Lesson 26: Two-Proportion z-Test

Calendar

Warm-Up

Ruck: Same Cadets

Do new boots lower 12 mile ruck times? Eight randomly selected cadets rucked once in their old boots and once in new boots (hours). Test at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Cadet 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8
Old 3.05 2.88 3.21 2.79 3.12 2.95 3.30 2.84
New 2.94 2.85 3.06 2.80 2.98 2.87 3.15 2.76
\(d\) = New \(-\) Old -0.11 -0.03 -0.15 0.01 -0.14 -0.08 -0.15 -0.08
TipAnswer

1. Hypotheses: \(H_0: \mu_D = 0\) versus \(H_a: \mu_D < 0\). \(\mu_D\) is the true mean change in ruck time (new minus old boots) for all cadets. Faster in new boots means \(< 0\).

2. Conditions: Paired, same cadets twice. Random sample of cadets. \(n = 8 \le 40\), so the differences must be approximately normal.

3. Test statistic:

\[t = \frac{\bar{d} - \Delta_0}{s_D/\sqrt{n}}\]

\[\bar{d} = -0.0912, \qquad s_D = 0.0584, \qquad t = \frac{-0.0912 - 0}{0.0584/\sqrt{8}} = \mathbf{-4.42}, \qquad \text{df} = 7\]

4. P-value: \(P(T \le -4.42) = \mathbf{0.0015}\). pt(-4.418, 7)

5. Decision: \(0.0015 \le 0.05\). Reject \(H_0\).

6. Conclusion: At the 5% level there is sufficient evidence that the true mean 12 mile ruck time of cadets is lower in new boots than in old boots.

Ruck: Two Companies, Pass or Fail

Now change the design. The battalion issues new boots to every cadet in Company A and leaves Company B in old boots. Each cadet either finishes the 12 mile ruck in 3 hours or less (pass) or does not.

Company Cadets Passed
A: New boots 150 126
B: Old boots 140 103

Can we run the same test as above?

TipAnswer

No. Two things changed.

  • Two separate groups. No cadet is in both companies, so there are no pairs to subtract.
  • Pass or fail. Each cadet gives a yes or a no, not a time. There is no mean or standard deviation to compute. The parameter is a proportion in each group.

Two independent groups and a yes/no outcome: compare \(p_1\) and \(p_2\). That is today. See The Steps below.

What We Did: Lessons 18 to 25

NoteReview: Central Limit Theorem (Lesson 18)
  • \(\bar{X} \sim N\left(\mu,\ \left(\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^2\right)\), approximately, for \(n > 40\)
  • Normal population: exact for any \(n\)
  • pnorm(x, mu, sigma/sqrt(n))
  • \(\hat{p} \sim N\left(p,\ \dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}\right)\), approximately, when \(np \ge 10\) and \(n(1-p) \ge 10\)
NoteReview: Confidence Intervals I (Lesson 19)
  • \(\text{estimate} \pm \text{critical value} \times \text{standard error}\)
  • Mean, \(n > 40\): \(\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\)
  • Proportion, \(n\hat{p} \ge 10\) and \(n(1-\hat{p}) \ge 10\): \(\hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}\)
  • qnorm(0.975) for 95%, qnorm(0.95) for 90%, qnorm(0.995) for 99%
  • 95% confident describes the method
NoteReview: Confidence Intervals II (Lesson 20)
  • Small sample, normal population: \(\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\,\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\)
  • \(\text{df} = n - 1\), qt(0.975, n - 1) for 95%
  • \(t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1} > z_{\alpha/2}\), so the \(t\) interval is wider
  • Sample size for width \(w\): \(n = \left(\dfrac{2\,z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{w}\right)^2\), rounded up
NoteReview: Intro to Hypothesis Testing (Lesson 21)
  • \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\) versus \(H_a\): \(\mu < \mu_0\), \(\mu > \mu_0\), or \(\mu \ne \mu_0\)
  • \(z = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}\), random sample, \(n > 40\)
  • P-value: pnorm(z), 1 - pnorm(z), 2 * pnorm(-abs(z))
  • Reject \(H_0\) if the P-value is at or below \(\alpha\), otherwise fail to reject
  • Type I: reject a true \(H_0\) (\(\alpha\)). Type II: fail to reject a false \(H_0\) (\(\beta\))
  • Fail to reject is not accept
NoteReview: One-Sample t-Test (Lesson 22)
  • \(t = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}\), \(\text{df} = n - 1\)
  • Random sample, \(n \le 40\), population approximately normal
  • P-value: pt(t, n - 1), 1 - pt(t, n - 1), 2 * pt(-abs(t), n - 1)
  • t.test(x, mu = mu0, alternative = "two.sided")
NoteReview: One-Proportion z-Test (Lesson 23)
  • \(z = \dfrac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1 - p_0)/n}}\)
  • Random sample, \(np_0 \ge 10\) and \(n(1 - p_0) \ge 10\)
  • The test uses \(p_0\), the interval uses \(\hat{p}\)
  • Statistically significant does not mean practically significant
NoteReview: Two-Sample t-Test (Lesson 24)
  • \(t = \dfrac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - \Delta_0}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}}\), approximate df \(\nu\), rounded down
  • Two independent random samples, both populations approximately normal (or both \(n > 40\))
  • Interval: \((\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,\nu}\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}\)
  • Name group 1 first, then put the claim in \(H_a\)
  • t.test(x1, x2, alternative = "greater")
NoteReview: Paired t-Test (Lesson 25)
  • Paired data: two measurements on the same unit. Analyze \(d_i\).
  • \(t = \dfrac{\bar{d} - \Delta_0}{s_D/\sqrt{n}}\), \(\text{df} = n - 1\), a one-sample \(t\) on the differences
  • Interval: \(\bar{d} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\dfrac{s_D}{\sqrt{n}}\)
  • Pairing removes unit to unit variability, so the standard error shrinks
  • t.test(x, y, paired = TRUE, alternative = "greater")

The Takeaway for Today

NoteKey Concepts from Lesson 26
  • Two proportions: two independent groups, a yes/no outcome. Estimate \(p_1 - p_2\) with \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2\).
  • Test statistic: \(z = \dfrac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}\), pooled \(\hat{p} = \dfrac{x_1+x_2}{n_1+n_2}\)
  • Conditions: two independent random samples, \(n_i\hat{p}_i \ge 10\) and \(n_i(1-\hat{p}_i) \ge 10\) in both groups
  • Interval: \((\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1}+\dfrac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}\), not pooled
  • The test pools, the interval does not.

Two-Proportion \(z\) Test

ImportantTwo-Proportion \(z\) Test for \(p_1 - p_2\)

\[\hat{p}_1 = \frac{x_1}{n_1}, \qquad \hat{p}_2 = \frac{x_2}{n_2}, \qquad \text{pooled } \hat{p} = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2}\]

\[z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1}+\dfrac{1}{n_2}\right)}}\]

  • Hypotheses: \(H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0\) versus \(H_a\): \(p_1 - p_2 > 0\), \(< 0\), or \(\ne 0\)
  • Conditions: two independent random samples; \(n_1\hat{p}_1\), \(n_1(1-\hat{p}_1)\), \(n_2\hat{p}_2\), \(n_2(1-\hat{p}_2)\) all \(\ge 10\)
  • If \(H_0\) is true, \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\), approximately
\(H_a\) P-value
\(p_1 - p_2 > 0\) 1 - pnorm(z)
\(p_1 - p_2 < 0\) pnorm(z)
\(p_1 - p_2 \ne 0\) 2 * pnorm(-abs(z))
ImportantConfidence Interval for \(p_1 - p_2\)

\[(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}\]

  • Same conditions as the test
  • Interval contains \(0\): no evidence of a difference. All positive: \(p_1 > p_2\). All negative: \(p_1 < p_2\).
WarningThe Test Pools, the Interval Does Not

The test assumes \(H_0: p_1 = p_2\), so both groups share one proportion, and our best estimate of it combines both samples: the pooled \(\hat{p}\). The interval assumes nothing about \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), so each group keeps its own \(\hat{p}_i\). Same idea as L23: the test uses what \(H_0\) says, the interval uses the data.

The Steps

Ruck: Two Companies

Back to the Warm-Up. Is the pass rate higher in new boots? Test at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Company \(n\) \(x\) (passed) \(\hat{p}\)
1: A, New boots 150 126 0.8400
2: B, Old boots 140 103 0.7357

1. Hypotheses: State \(H_0\) and \(H_a\).

TipAnswer

\[H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0\]

\[H_a: p_1 - p_2 > 0\]

\(p_1\) and \(p_2\) are the true proportions of all cadets who finish the 12 mile ruck in 3 hours or less in new boots and in old boots. Higher in new boots means \(> 0\).

2. Conditions: What conditions allow us to do this?

TipAnswer

Two independent random samples: different cadets in each company.

\[n_1\hat{p}_1 = 126, \quad n_1(1-\hat{p}_1) = 24, \quad n_2\hat{p}_2 = 103, \quad n_2(1-\hat{p}_2) = 37\]

All \(\ge 10\). These are just the counts of passes and fails in each group.

3. Test statistic: Compute the pooled \(\hat{p}\) and \(z\).

TipAnswer

\[\hat{p} = \frac{126 + 103}{150 + 140} = \frac{229}{290} = 0.7897\]

\[z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1}+\dfrac{1}{n_2}\right)}}\]

\[z = \frac{0.8400 - 0.7357}{\sqrt{0.7897(0.2103)\left(\dfrac{1}{150}+\dfrac{1}{140}\right)}} = \frac{0.1043}{0.0479} = \mathbf{2.18}\]

4. P-value: Find it.

TipAnswer

\[P(Z \ge 2.18) = \mathbf{0.0147}\]

1 - pnorm(2.178)

5. Decision: Reject or fail to reject?

TipAnswer

\(0.0147 \le 0.05\). Reject \(H_0\).

6. Conclusion: State it in context.

TipAnswer

Since the P-value \(0.0147 \le \alpha = 0.05\), reject \(H_0\). At the 5% level there is sufficient evidence that the true proportion of cadets who finish the 12 mile ruck in 3 hours or less is higher in new boots than in old boots.

Confidence interval: Build a 95% interval for \(p_1 - p_2\) and interpret it.

TipAnswer

Not pooled: each group keeps its own \(\hat{p}_i\).

\[\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.8400(0.1600)}{150}+\frac{0.7357(0.2643)}{140}} = 0.0478\]

\[0.1043 \pm 1.96(0.0478) = (0.011,\ 0.198)\]

We are 95% confident that the true pass rate in new boots is between 1.1 and 19.8 percentage points higher than in old boots. All positive, consistent with rejecting \(H_0\).

Board Problems

Problem 1: Name the Test

NoteQuestions

Choose from: \(z\) test for a mean, one-sample \(t\), one-proportion \(z\), two-sample \(t\), paired \(t\), two-proportion \(z\).

  1. Do fewer than 30% of cadets drink coffee daily? \(250\) cadets surveyed.

  2. Is the proportion of cadets who pass the swim test on the first attempt different for recruited athletes and non-athletes? \(120\) of each.

  3. \(18\) cadets take a reaction time test once rested and once after a 24 hour field exercise.

  4. Mean GPA of \(25\) cadets from each of two regiments.

  5. Mean push-up count of \(60\) cadets compared to the standard of \(50\).

TipAnswers

  1. One-proportion \(z\). One group, yes/no, compared to \(0.30\).

  2. Two-proportion \(z\). Two independent groups, yes/no.

  3. Paired \(t\). Same cadets twice.

  4. Two-sample \(t\). Two independent groups, a numeric outcome.

  5. \(z\) test for a mean. One group, \(n > 40\), use \(s\) for \(\sigma\).

Problem 2: Air Assault

Cadets who attended an optional prep course and cadets who did not were each randomly sampled from those who started Air Assault School. Is the graduation rate different? Test at \(\alpha = 0.10\).

Group Started Graduated
1: Prep course 60 41
2: No prep 58 30
NoteQuestions

  1. State the hypotheses.

  2. Check the conditions.

  3. Compute \(\hat{p}_1\), \(\hat{p}_2\), the pooled \(\hat{p}\), and \(z\).

  4. Find the P-value.

  5. Decide and conclude.

  6. Build and interpret a 90% interval for \(p_1 - p_2\).

TipAnswers
  1. \[H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0\]

\[H_a: p_1 - p_2 \ne 0\]

\(p_1\) and \(p_2\) are the true graduation rates of cadets with and without the prep course.

  1. Two independent random samples. \(41\), \(19\), \(30\), \(28\) all \(\ge 10\).

  2. \[\hat{p}_1 = \frac{41}{60} = 0.6833, \qquad \hat{p}_2 = \frac{30}{58} = 0.5172, \qquad \hat{p} = \frac{71}{118} = 0.6017\]

\[z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1}+\dfrac{1}{n_2}\right)}}\]

\[z = \frac{0.6833 - 0.5172}{\sqrt{0.6017(0.3983)\left(\frac{1}{60}+\frac{1}{58}\right)}} = \frac{0.1661}{\sqrt{0.6017(0.3983)\left(\frac{1}{60}+\frac{1}{58}\right)}} = \frac{0.1661}{0.0901} = \mathbf{1.84}\]

  1. \[2P(Z \ge 1.84) = \mathbf{0.0654}\]

2 * pnorm(-abs(1.843))

  1. Since the P-value \(0.0654 \le \alpha = 0.10\), reject \(H_0\). At the 10% level there is sufficient evidence that the true Air Assault graduation rate differs between cadets with and without the prep course.

  2. \(z_{0.05} = 1.645\). qnorm(0.95)

\[0.1661 \pm 1.645(0.0889) = (0.020,\ 0.312)\]

We are 90% confident that the true graduation rate with the prep course is between 2 and 31.2 percentage points higher than without it. All positive, consistent with rejecting at \(\alpha = 0.10\).

Problem 3: Read the Output

During Cadet Basic Training, one company’s barracks had new air filters and another’s did not. A cadet counts as a sick call if they went to sick call at least once. Group 1 is the filtered barracks.


    2-sample test for equality of proportions without continuity correction

data:  c(18, 31) out of c(220, 230)
X-squared = 3.2508, df = 1, p-value = 0.03569
alternative hypothesis: less
95 percent confidence interval:
 -1.000000000 -0.005051453
sample estimates:
    prop 1     prop 2 
0.08181818 0.13478261
NoteQuestions

  1. What are \(H_0\) and \(H_a\)? What claim is being tested?

  2. Report \(\hat{p}_1\), \(\hat{p}_2\), \(z\), and the P-value.

  3. Decide at \(\alpha = 0.05\) and conclude in context.

  4. Is the printed interval the two-sided 95% interval for \(p_1 - p_2\)? Why or why not?

TipAnswers

  1. \(H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0\) versus \(H_a: p_1 - p_2 < 0\), from “alternative hypothesis: less.” The claim: the filtered barracks has a lower sick call rate.

  2. \(\hat{p}_1 = 0.0818\), \(\hat{p}_2 = 0.1348\). \(z = -\sqrt{3.2508} = -1.80\), negative because \(\hat{p}_1 < \hat{p}_2\). P-value \(= 0.0357\).

  3. \(0.0357 \le 0.05\). Reject \(H_0\). At the 5% level there is sufficient evidence that the true proportion of cadets who go to sick call is lower in barracks with new air filters.

  4. No. A one-sided test prints a one-sided bound, here \((-1, -0.0051)\). The two-sided interval is \((-0.110,\ 0.004)\).

Problem 4: Sapper Leader Course

From one company, \(9\) of \(12\) cadets who attended Sapper Leader Course graduated. From another, \(6\) of \(10\) graduated. A classmate wants to run a two-proportion \(z\) test.

NoteQuestions

  1. Check the conditions.

  2. Can we use the two-proportion \(z\) test here?

TipAnswers

  1. \(n_1\hat{p}_1 = 9\), \(n_1(1-\hat{p}_1) = 3\), \(n_2\hat{p}_2 = 6\), \(n_2(1-\hat{p}_2) = 4\). None reach \(10\).

  2. No. The samples are too small for the normal approximation, so the \(z\) P-value is not trustworthy. We would need more cadets in each group.
