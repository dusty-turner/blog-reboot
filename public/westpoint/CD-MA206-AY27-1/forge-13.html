Example Problem

A researcher measures 333 penguins at Palmer Station: body mass (g), flipper length (mm), sex, and bill length (mm). She fits the model below.

fit <- lm(body_mass ~ flipper_len + sex + bill_len, data = penguins)
summary(fit)

  1. Write the estimated regression equation.

\(\widehat{\text{mass}} = -5433.5 + 48.21(\text{flipper}) + 358.6(\text{male}) -5.20(\text{bill})\), where male is 1 for a male and 0 for a female.

  1. Interpret the coefficient of flipper_len.

Holding sex and bill length fixed, each additional millimeter of flipper length is associated with an increase of about 48.2 g in average body mass.

  1. Interpret the coefficient of sexmale.

Holding flipper length and bill length fixed, males weigh about 358.6 g more than females on average.

  1. Which predictors are significant at \(\alpha = 0.05\)? How do you know?
  • flipper_len: significant. \(t = 26.18\), P-value \(< 0.001\).
  • sexmale: significant. \(t = 8.63\), P-value \(< 0.001\).
  • bill_len: not significant. \(t = -1.07\), P-value \(= 0.285\) \(> 0.05\). Once flipper length and sex are in the model, bill length adds no detectable information about body mass.
  1. What percentage of the variation in body mass does the model explain?

\(R^2 = 0.807\), so about 80.7%.

  1. Assess each of the regression assumptions.
  • L: residuals vs fitted. The red line curves up at the low end: the lightest predicted penguins sit above the line. A mild concern; the model may be missing something (species, for example).
  • I: not something a plot shows. Each penguin is a different bird measured once, so reasonable.
  • N: Q-Q plot. The points hug the line, slightly off in the tails. Reasonable.
  • E: residuals vs fitted. About the same spread across the fitted values, no fan. Reasonable.

male <- penguins$body_mass[penguins$sex == "male"]

t.test(mass, mu = 4000, alternative = "two.sided")
